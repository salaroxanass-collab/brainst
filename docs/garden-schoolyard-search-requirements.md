# BrainStorm Search Requirements: Private Gardens and Schoolyards

This document defines the requirement vocabulary that the BrainStorming Engine should understand. It is intentionally broader than a list of design styles: people often describe a need, problem, feeling, activity, constraint, material, user or maintenance concern rather than naming a landscape typology.

The phrases below are examples and synonyms, not rigid commands. One search can contain several requirements, and the engine should retain all of them when ranking references.

## Shared information to extract from every search

- **Place:** country, region, city, rural/suburban/urban setting, coastal/inland/mountain location and local landscape character.
- **Climate:** hot, dry, arid, Mediterranean, temperate, continental, tropical, humid, windy, coastal, alpine, cold, frost-prone, drought-prone or flood-prone.
- **Site:** dimensions, area, orientation, slope, soil, existing vegetation, views, neighbouring buildings, access, utilities, contamination and heritage features.
- **Time:** historical period, design movement, completion date, seasonal use, immediate effect or long-term establishment.
- **Users:** ages, group sizes, mobility, sensory needs, cultural expectations, pets, supervision and frequency of use.
- **Priorities:** beauty, use, ecology, comfort, learning, play, food, status, restoration, resilience, privacy, safety or low maintenance.
- **Resources:** budget, phasing, available labour, irrigation, local materials, specialist maintenance and desired lifespan.
- **Evidence:** built project, verified source, plans, sections, planting plan, construction detail, cost information, performance data or post-occupancy evaluation.

## How the engine should use this vocabulary

1. Extract multiple requirements rather than collapsing the request into one keyword.
2. Separate **hard constraints** (location, date, age, accessibility, site size, budget) from **preferences** (mood, style, materials).
3. Return exact matches first, then clearly labelled adjacent precedents that solve the same design problem.
4. Never silently replace a requested country, period, school age or accessibility requirement with a different one.
5. Explain why each precedent matches using the user’s own requirements.
6. State which requirements are not evidenced by the source rather than inventing information.
7. Prefer built, verifiable projects with a project-page link, correct photographs and credited designers.
8. Allow natural combinations such as “small shaded Italian courtyard for entertaining with a dog” or “low-budget primary schoolyard with nature play, inclusive access and flood storage.”
